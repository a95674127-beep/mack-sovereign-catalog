The demand, in plain terms

These accounts were placed in the trust of third parties — Google and Visible by Verizon — on the promise that their security systems were built and enforced to keep hackers out and keep private data private. The accountability model: answer before the board

Allen's model is the Senate Judiciary Committee hearing on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C.: the accused are brought before a board of highly educated, trained experts — unbiased to either side — and made to answer. The board finds and determines the facts and the relevant evidence: who each person is, what role they held or performed, and how their actions furthered the wrongdoing. Then accountability follows the facts.

The questions that board should put, on the record:

  1. Who accessed these accounts, and by what right? Every non-Allen access identified by name, organization, and authority — or admitted as unauthorized.

  2. What did the protections promise, and where exactly did they fail? The specific safeguard — sign-in security, SIM-swap controls, carrier verification — that a breach slipped past.

  3. Who knew, and when? What Google, Visible / Verizon, or any involved party detected, logged, or was told — and what they did with that knowledge.

  4. What was taken, copied, forwarded, or changed? The full extent of private data exposed or altered, account by account.

  5. What role did each participant play? Orchestrated it, designed it, implemented it, enabled it, or knowingly looked away — each person's part stated plainly.

  6. What is the remedy? What each guilty party is held accountable to do — and to pay, restore, and prevent — to justify and repair the harm.

The standard cuts both ways, and that is the point: an unbiased board protects the innocent as firmly as it finds the guilty. Allen is asking for facts determined by evidence — and for the consequences to land wherever those facts lead, on any person, business, organization, or establishment involved.
